This paper presents a model of temptation based on anticipatory utility. Temptation arises when an earlier self does not affectively anticipate a future consumption episode, which generates surprise utility for the consuming self that the decision-maker does not value prospectively. The resulting time-inconsistent preferences generate temptation spending shares that decrease with wealth and a behavioral poverty trap. Timing and planning provide soft self-control by allowing future non-temptation consumption to be anticipated before resources become liquid, while income risk raises temptation spending by preventing decision-makers from enjoying the anticipation of non-temptation consumption. Commitment devices provide hard self-control by determining how much future liquidity remains exposed to temptation from intervening selves. The interaction between anticipation and liquidity generates substitutability between soft and hard self-control and delivers implications for payment timing and frequency and the design of commitment devices.
