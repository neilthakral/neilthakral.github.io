Public-housing authorities restrict applicants' choices to balance prioritizing vulnerable households against preventing congestion. How do applicants' mistakes influence the effectiveness of these restrictions? We combine administrative vacancy data with a survey of 587 UK public-housing residents, estimating preferences via a choice experiment and eliciting behavioral biases. We compare two policies: the UK-mechanism batches arrivals and restricts acceptances; the US-mechanism restricts rejections. Under rationality, the US-mechanism reduces congestion but delivers smaller match-quality advantages for higher-priority applicants than the UK-mechanism. Bias affects the UK-mechanism more strongly overall. Some individual mistakes even improve social welfare by reducing congestion. The welfare ranking of the mechanisms therefore depends on applicant biases.
